Synopsis — What This Article Is About

After every major election in India, the same question rises like smoke from a fire that never quite goes out: Is BJP a political party, or is it a religious movement wearing a political costume? And if it is the latter — what does that mean for a country of 1.4 billion people, a fifth of whom are not Hindu?

This article is for anyone who has felt confused, unsettled, or simply curious about the BJP phenomenon — but lacked the political vocabulary to make sense of it. It does not require prior knowledge of Indian politics. It requires only a genuine interest in one of the most consequential questions in modern democracy: Can a nation of extraordinary diversity survive — and thrive — when its ruling party anchors its identity in a single religion?

We trace BJP's ideological roots from the RSS to Narendra Modi, separate the ancient philosophy of Hinduism from the 20th-century politics of Hindutva, examine what India's own Constitution demands, compare how other democracies handle the same tension, and — crucially — give a fair hearing to why millions of thoughtful Indians find BJP's project genuinely compelling. The answer, in the end, is not simple. But it matters enormously — for India, and for every democracy watching from the outside.

In the dense, humidity-soaked streets of Kolkata, polling booths have once again closed. West Bengal — a state that has produced some of India's finest secular intellectuals, that gave the world Rabindranath Tagore, that once championed left-wing politics for three unbroken decades — has voted in an atmosphere thick with tension, identity politics, and the unmistakable saffron of the Bharatiya Janata Party. Across tea stalls and WhatsApp groups, in drawing rooms and university corridors, across India and in every diaspora community around the world, the same question is rising: What happens when a political party wraps itself in the flag of God?

This is not a question for political experts alone. It never was. Whether religion belongs in the machinery of a modern democratic state is a question for citizens — for ordinary people who vote, who pray, who pay taxes, who raise children, and who simply want to live with dignity alongside those who believe differently. It is a question, in other words, that belongs to all of us.

Chapter One

Understanding BJP: A Brief Portrait of a Movement, Not Just a Party

The Bharatiya Janata Party is not simply a political party the way you might think of the Democrats or Republicans in America, or Labour and Conservatives in Britain. To understand BJP, you have to understand that it is the political arm of a much older, much larger ideological movement — a movement called Hindutva.

The BJP was formally founded in 1980, but its ideological roots go back much further — to 1925, when a man named Keshav Baliram Hedgewar founded the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) in the city of Nagpur. The RSS is a paramilitary volunteer organization built around the idea that India is, at its soul, a Hindu nation — and that the purpose of politics, culture, education, and even daily life should be to protect and advance that Hindu identity.

From the RSS grew a tree of affiliated organizations — schools, labor unions, student groups, tribal welfare bodies, and eventually political parties. The Jana Sangh, the BJP's direct predecessor, was the first major political vehicle. When the BJP emerged in 1980 under the leadership of Atal Bihari Vajpayee and L.K. Advani, it inherited this entire ideological apparatus. Today, the BJP-RSS ecosystem is one of the most sophisticated political machines in the world.

Hindutva and Hinduism are not the same word. One is a religion of 330 million gods, of meditative depth, of extraordinary tolerance. The other is a political weapon forged in the furnace of twentieth-century nationalism.

And here is the first and most critical distinction that most people — including many Hindus — do not fully grasp: Hindutva and Hinduism are not the same thing.

Hinduism is one of the world's oldest living religions. It encompasses extraordinary philosophical traditions — the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, the Vedas. It contains within itself traditions of non-violence, of cosmic humility, of deeply personal spirituality. It is a religion that, historically, did not proselytize, did not demand that the world convert, and coexisted with Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, and eventually Islam and Christianity on Indian soil for centuries.

Hindutva, on the other hand, is a political ideology invented in the 20th century. The word was coined by Vinayak Damodar Savarkar in his 1923 pamphlet, Hindutva: Who is a Hindu? Savarkar's vision was not religious at all — he was himself a self-described atheist. His vision was nationalist. He defined a Hindu not as someone who worships Hindu gods, but as someone whose "fatherland" and "holy land" are both in India. By this definition, Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, and Buddhists belong — but Muslims and Christians, whose holy lands are Mecca and Jerusalem, do not, regardless of how many generations their families have lived on Indian soil.

This is the ideology that courses through BJP's veins. This is what the post-election debate is actually about — not a religion, but a political doctrine that has borrowed religion's emotional power.

Chapter Two

The DNA of the BJP's Political Strategy: How Religion Became a Vote Machine

To understand why religious politics works — not just in India, but everywhere — you need to understand something fundamental about human psychology: identity is more powerful than policy.

When a political party tells you that the economy will grow by 7%, your brain processes that as a policy claim — abstract, uncertain, maybe true, maybe not. But when a political party tells you that your civilization, your culture, your ancestors, your gods are under threat, something far deeper activates. Fear. Tribal loyalty. The ancient primal instinct to protect what is sacred.

BJP, and specifically its most successful political tactician Narendra Modi, mastered this emotional calculus with extraordinary precision. The 2014 general election that brought Modi to power was run not primarily on development slogans — though those existed — but on a deep undercurrent of cultural pride and perceived Hindu victimhood. The message, sometimes explicit and sometimes encoded in symbol and rhetoric, was: after centuries of Muslim invasions and decades of Congress-led "appeasement," Hindus must reclaim their rightful place in their own land.

Key Ideological Milestones

1923: Savarkar publishes "Hindutva," laying the philosophical foundation for Hindu nationalism as a political rather than religious concept.

1925: RSS founded by K.B. Hedgewar in Nagpur as a cadre-based volunteer organization to build Hindu cultural unity.

1948: Mahatma Gandhi assassinated by Nathuram Godse, an RSS affiliate who believed Gandhi was "appeasing" Muslims.

1980: BJP formally established, positioning itself as a "party with a difference" — the difference being explicit Hindu cultural nationalism.

1992: Babri Masjid demolition by a Hindu mob in Ayodhya, an event in which senior BJP leaders were present — a watershed moment in modern Indian communal history.

2002: Gujarat riots under Chief Minister Narendra Modi's watch — over 1,000 people, mostly Muslims, killed. Human Rights Watch called it a "pogrom."

2014–Present: BJP under Modi wins two consecutive historic majorities, transforming India's political, cultural, and institutional landscape.

In West Bengal, BJP found a particularly fertile ground for this strategy. The state has a roughly 27% Muslim population — one of the highest in India outside the Northeast and Jammu & Kashmir. Bengal also has a long history of Hindu-Muslim tension, including the devastating Great Partition of 1947 that cleaved Bengal in two, sending millions of Hindu refugees streaming into West Bengal from what became East Pakistan. The psychological wounds of Partition — the memories of violence, of displacement, of loss — have never fully healed. BJP has been extraordinarily skilled at reopening them.

In every Bengali village where BJP campaigned, the message was carefully calibrated: your culture is endangered, your temples are under threat, your daughters are unsafe, your population is being "replaced." These are deeply coded messages, not always spoken aloud, but understood perfectly by their intended audience.

Chapter Three

What Does India's Own Constitution Say? The Secular Compact That BJP Tests Daily

Here is something remarkable that most people don't know: India's founding document — the Constitution of India, written largely by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar and adopted in 1950 — is one of the most explicitly secular constitutions in the world. In 1976, during Indira Gandhi's tenure, the 42nd Constitutional Amendment made it even more explicit, adding the word "secular" directly into the Preamble.

The Preamble of the Indian Constitution today reads: "We, the people of India, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic..."

Indian secularism is, however, a distinct concept from Western secularism. In France, secularism (laïcité) means the strict exclusion of religion from the public sphere. In India, secularism has traditionally meant something different: equal respect for all religions, and the equal protection of all religious communities by the state. The state would not favor Hinduism, but it would also not be hostile to it. It would protect Muslim personal law, fund Hindu temples, support Sikh gurdwaras, and recognize Christian educational institutions — all equally, all simultaneously.

India's Constitution was written by a man — Ambedkar — who was himself an untouchable, who was humiliated by Hindu caste hierarchy his entire life, and who eventually converted to Buddhism. He built a document not for any one faith, but for all of humanity living within India's borders.

BJP's political project represents a fundamental challenge to this constitutional compact. The BJP's stated vision of India — a "Hindu Rashtra" or Hindu Nation — is in direct tension with a constitution that explicitly protects all faiths equally. When BJP leaders speak of restoring a Hindu cultural identity to the Indian state, they are not merely speaking of policy. They are speaking of rewriting the moral architecture of the republic itself.

This is not an accusation. It is a statement of ideological fact that BJP's own intellectual traditions openly acknowledge. The RSS has never hidden its admiration for a mono-cultural national identity. The question is whether, and how far, BJP will take this project in practice.

Chapter Four

The Deeper Problem: Why Religion in Politics Is Dangerous — Everywhere, Always

Let us step back from India entirely for a moment. Because the problem of religious politics is not Indian, not Hindu, not even Asian. It is a universal human problem, and history has given us devastating case studies on every continent.

The core danger of religious politics is this: religion, by its nature, cannot be debated away.

If a politician tells you that the government's economic policy is wrong, you can argue with numbers. You can show GDP data, unemployment figures, trade balances. Policy is falsifiable. Policy can be corrected.

But if a politician tells you that God has ordained a particular social order — that the nation belongs to one religious community, that another community is alien or dangerous — you cannot argue with God. Faith is, by definition, beyond reason. When politics becomes theology, the opposition becomes not merely wrong, but sinful. The minority becomes not merely a different voter bloc, but a threat to the divine order. And in that space, violence becomes not just possible, but righteous.

A Pattern Repeated Across History

1930s Germany: The Nazi movement positioned itself as a defense of "Christian German civilization" against Jewish "cultural contamination." The result was the Holocaust — the systematic murder of six million Jews.

1990s Yugoslavia: Serbian Orthodox nationalism used religious identity to justify ethnic cleansing of Bosnian Muslims and Croatian Catholics. The Srebrenica massacre — 8,000 Muslims killed in three days — was the worst atrocity in Europe since World War II.

Myanmar, 2017: Buddhist nationalist monks helped build the political climate that led to the genocide of the Rohingya Muslim minority — a people who had lived in Burma for generations.

Saudi Arabia, ongoing: A state built explicitly on Sunni Islamic law cannot offer full citizenship to Shia Muslims, Jews, atheists, or women who wish to live as equals. It can only offer degrees of tolerance that it can rescind at will.

The pattern is always the same: when the state adopts a religious identity, those outside that religion — even if born on the same soil, even if their families have been there for a thousand years — become guests, not citizens.

India has had its own searing experiences of this dynamic. The 2002 Gujarat riots, in which over a thousand people — predominantly Muslims — were killed in organized violence, remain one of the darkest chapters in post-independence Indian history. Human Rights Watch documented the violence in excruciating detail, including evidence of police inaction and, in some documented cases, police complicity. The chief minister of Gujarat at the time was Narendra Modi.

This is not mentioned to condemn BJP voters — most BJP voters are ordinary, decent people who voted for development, for national pride, for a strong leader in a chaotic world. But it is mentioned because the institutional record matters. It is the record of what religious nationalism, in the wrong circumstances, can become.

Chapter Five

The Fair Argument: What BJP Supporters Actually Believe — And Why It Has Genuine Appeal

A serious analysis must be fair. And fairness requires acknowledging that BJP's rise is not simply a story of cynical manipulation. Millions of educated, thoughtful, economically aspirational Indians have voted BJP with genuine conviction, not just communal passion. What do they believe?

First, they believe that the Congress Party — India's dominant party from independence until 2014 — practiced a form of cynical pseudo-secularism that actually undermined genuine religious equality. Congress, in this view, would codify Muslim personal law exemptions (like the Shah Bano case in 1986) not out of principled pluralism but out of naked vote-bank politics. Meanwhile, Hindu religious sites like temples remained under government control and their revenues redirected by the state — a form of regulation that mosques and churches did not face. This asymmetry, BJP voters argue, was not secularism. It was Hindu discrimination dressed as tolerance.

This is a legitimate grievance. The asymmetry is real. India's approach to different religious community laws has been inconsistent, and that inconsistency has caused genuine resentment.

Second, BJP supporters argue that Hindu culture — as a civilization, not just a religion — has been systematically denigrated by post-independence India's English-educated, left-leaning intellectual elite. That yoga, Sanskrit, Ayurveda, and ancient Indian philosophy were treated as superstition rather than as a rich intellectual inheritance. That textbooks underrepresented Hindu achievements and overrepresented Mughal history. That there was a fashionable self-loathing among India's elite that treated anything rooted in Hindu tradition as backward.

This, too, has some merit. Cultural pride is legitimate. A civilization that produced the mathematics of Aryabhata, the philosophy of Adi Shankaracharya, the architecture of Hampi and Khajuraho, the literature of Kalidasa — has every right to be proud.

The problem is not Hindu pride. The problem is when pride becomes supremacy. When the celebration of one's heritage requires the diminishment of another's.

Third, BJP supporters argue that India faces genuine security threats — from cross-border terrorism, from radicalized elements within the Muslim community, from geopolitical pressures from Pakistan and China — and that the Congress Party was weak, naive, and corrupt in its responses. Modi, in this view, represents strength, decisiveness, and national self-respect.

Again — there is a legitimate argument here. Security is a real issue. National self-respect is not inherently dangerous.

The problem is not in these grievances individually. The problem is in the instrument chosen to address them. When you address legitimate policy asymmetries by building an ideology of Hindu supremacy, you solve one injustice by creating a larger one. When you channel cultural pride into othering of minorities, you poison the well from which all of India — Hindu and Muslim and Christian and Sikh — must drink.

Chapter Six

How Other Democracies Handle the Religion-Politics Line

France Laïcité (Hard Secularism) Religion strictly private. No religious symbols in government buildings or public schools. Strong — but creates tension with Muslim minority over hijab bans.

Turkey Secular → Islamic drift Erdoğan's AKP has progressively moved Turkey toward political Islam, eroding Atatürk's secular republic. Cautionary — judiciary compromised, press restricted, minorities threatened.

The global evidence is clear: the moment a modern democratic state ties its identity to a single religion, a predictable sequence begins. First come symbolic moves — renaming cities, rewriting textbooks, promoting cultural symbols of the dominant faith in public spaces. Then come legal asymmetries — laws that apply differently to different religious communities. Then, gradually, institutional erosion — courts, press, civil society bodies that challenge the religious-nationalist project are delegitimized, defunded, or captured. And at the end of that road, in the worst cases, is violence.

No country has ever successfully built a healthy, lasting democracy on a foundation of religious exclusivity. Not one. This is not a partisan opinion. It is the verdict of history.

Chapter Seven

What BJP Has Actually Done in Power: Beyond the Rhetoric

Between 2014 and today, BJP has been in national power for over a decade. What has it actually done? The record is complex — neither as benign as supporters claim, nor as apocalyptic as some critics charge.

On the positive side: BJP has implemented genuine development programs. The Jan Dhan financial inclusion initiative brought banking access to hundreds of millions of poor Indians. The Ujjwala Yojana distributed cooking gas cylinders to rural households, dramatically reducing indoor air pollution. Infrastructure investment — roads, railways, highways — accelerated. Digital India was a real initiative with real impact. Swachh Bharat (Clean India) built over 100 million toilets, addressing a genuine public health and dignity crisis.

These are real achievements. A government can do real good for people while simultaneously advancing an ideological project that threatens democratic norms. Both things can be true at once.

On the concerning side, the list is also long and documented:

The Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA) of 2019 granted a path to Indian citizenship for refugees from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan — but explicitly excluded Muslims. It was the first time in India's history that religion was made a criterion for citizenship. The Supreme Court has not yet definitively ruled on its constitutionality.

The revocation of Article 370 in Jammu & Kashmir — which removed the Muslim-majority state's special constitutional status — was done without the consent of the state assembly and under conditions of a total communications blackout. Whatever the policy merits, the process signaled that constitutional norms could be overridden when the BJP's ideological project required it.

The National Register of Citizens (NRC) in Assam, a process intended to identify illegal immigrants, effectively put the citizenship of nearly two million people — many of them poor, rural, and Muslim — in question, creating conditions of legal limbo and humanitarian anxiety.

Press freedom indices have declined sharply under BJP governance. India ranked 159th out of 180 countries in the 2023 Reporters Without Borders Press Freedom Index — a dramatic fall from 140th in 2014. Journalists covering communal violence, government corruption, or minority issues face arrest, harassment, and in some cases, assassination.

Mob lynching of Muslims — often on suspicion of carrying or consuming beef — has become a documented, recurring phenomenon under BJP governance. In most cases, perpetrators have faced limited prosecution. In some cases, BJP politicians have publicly honored the accused.

These are not normal policy disagreements. These are structural threats to the architecture of a plural democracy.

Chapter Eight

West Bengal: Why This State Matters So Much to the BJP Project

West Bengal is not just another state to BJP. It is a symbolic prize of enormous importance — and a genuine test of its ideology.

Bengal was the intellectual heart of India's independence movement. It was the birthplace of the Bengal Renaissance — the 19th century flowering of literature, philosophy, social reform, and modern thought that included figures like Raja Ram Mohan Roy, Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, Swami Vivekananda, and Rabindranath Tagore. Bengal produced a tradition of questioning, of humanist universalism, of rigorous intellectual engagement with ideas — including religious ideas — that was uniquely its own.

Tagore himself — the first non-European Nobel Laureate, the man who wrote the national anthems of both India and Bangladesh — was deeply suspicious of narrow nationalism. His 1916 lectures, published as Nationalism, warned that nationalism was a "great epidemic of evil" that had been "sweeping over the human world of the present age." He resigned his knighthood after the Jallianwala Bagh massacre. He believed in a civilization, not a nation-state — and certainly not a religious state.

"Where the mind is without fear and the head is held high — into that heaven of freedom, my Father, let my country awake."

— Rabindranath Tagore, Gitanjali (1912) — India's most beloved prayer for its own democracy

For BJP to win Bengal is not just about seats in Parliament. It is about defeating this tradition — about planting a saffron flag in the spiritual homeland of Indian humanism. The fact that the BJP has made significant electoral inroads in Bengal — from near-zero seats in 2014 to 77 of 294 assembly seats in 2021, before being defeated by Mamata Banerjee's Trinamool Congress — is therefore significant, whatever one thinks of Mamata's own governance record.

Chapter Nine

There Is a Better Way: What a Truly Confident Hindu India Would Look Like

Let us be clear about one thing: the problem is not Hindu pride. The problem is Hindu insecurity weaponized as political supremacy.

A truly confident Hindu civilization — one that is genuinely proud of what it has given the world — does not need to exclude Muslims from citizenship to feel secure. It does not need to rename streets and cities to feel historically vindicated. It does not need to lynch people over what they eat to protect its culture. Hinduism, at its philosophical height, was so capacious, so intellectually generous, so cosmically vast, that it had room for 330 million gods — surely it has room for 200 million Muslims.

Swami Vivekananda — the greatest Hindu cultural ambassador of the modern era, the man who stood at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago in 1893 and electrified the world — did not preach the exclusion of non-Hindus from India's public life. He preached universal brotherhood rooted in the deepest Hindu philosophical traditions. "I am proud to belong to a religion," he said, "which has taught the world both tolerance and universal acceptance."

That is the Hinduism worth defending. Not a Hinduism defined by what it is against — against Muslims, against Christians, against Pakistan, against "appeasement" — but a Hinduism defined by what it is for: wisdom, tolerance, cosmic humility, and the astonishing intellectual richness of a civilization that has been thinking seriously about the nature of consciousness and reality for five thousand years.

Any political movement that claims to represent Hinduism while practicing exclusion, fear-mongering, and communal violence has not elevated Hinduism. It has diminished it.

Final Chapter

The Verdict: What Thoughtful Citizens Sense, and Why That Instinct Deserves Trust

Those who feel unsettled after watching the West Bengal results — who cannot quite articulate the discomfort but feel it viscerally — are sensing something real. That instinct is sound. What is being contested is not just who wins or loses an election, but what kind of country India will be in twenty years.

Will India be a country where a Muslim family in Kolkata, a Christian family in Kerala, a Sikh family in Punjab can all say with equal confidence, "This is my country, and my government protects me as fully as it protects my Hindu neighbor"? Or will India become a country where some citizens are treated as full members of the national family and others as guests who must earn their place by cultural submission?

That is the question. It is not a question about whether you like Modi's economic policies, or whether Congress was corrupt, or whether Pakistan is a threat. Those are all separate, legitimate debates. The question is about the foundational architecture of the republic. And that architecture, once damaged, is extraordinarily difficult to repair.

History is full of countries that chose the road of religious nationalism with genuine popular enthusiasm — and arrived, decades later, at outcomes that their own citizens found unrecognizable and horrifying. History is also full of countries that chose to hold the harder, messier, more demanding road of genuine pluralism — and built, over time, something durable and worth living in.

India has already built one of the world's great democratic experiments. It has managed, imperfectly but genuinely, to hold together 1.4 billion people of astonishing diversity across seventy-five years of independence. The Constitution that Ambedkar gave India — written by a man who had every reason to hate the caste-Hindu establishment and chose instead to write a document protecting everyone equally — is a gift of almost unimaginable generosity.

The only question that matters now is whether India's citizens — Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, secular, devout, old and young — will choose to protect that gift, or allow it to be traded away, piece by piece, for the cheaper, easier, and ultimately destructive comfort of religious belonging in the machinery of state.

A Note on Balance & Perspective

This article does not argue that all BJP voters are communalists, that Hindu culture needs no defense, or that Congress was without fault. India's political reality is vastly complex. Millions of BJP voters are decent, thoughtful people motivated by legitimate aspirations — development, security, cultural pride, clean governance.

This article argues specifically against the principle that any democratic state should encode a single religion as its defining identity — because history, philosophy, and constitutional theory all point in the same direction: it ends badly for everyone, including the majority in whose name the project is pursued.

The conversation unfolding across India — in homes, classrooms, newsrooms, and ballot booths — is one of the most important of our time. Bring to it what Tagore, Ambedkar, and Vivekananda all brought to their greatest work: not fear, but courage. Not exclusion, but inquiry. Not tribal certainty, but hard-won wisdom.
